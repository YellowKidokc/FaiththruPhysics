# The Jurisdiction Test

## Pre-Test Thesis

This document records the pre-test form of the Death/Jurisdiction argument before adversarial review, Lean formalization, or empirical/historical testing.

The claim is not that a miracle, by itself, proves God.

A miracle may show power. Power alone is ambiguous. It could be deception, technology, unknown physics, simulation control, or brute force over a local constraint.

The stronger test is jurisdiction.

God is shown, if He is shown by resurrection, not by a rule being randomly broken, but by the deepest rule of the fallen system meeting the one person it has no rightful authority to bind.

## Core Statement

Death is not only biological cessation. In the Christian frame, death is the final jurisdictional claim of the corrupted order.

Sin gives death its rightful claim.

If there is sin, death has jurisdiction.

If there is corruption, death has jurisdiction.

If a person belongs to the fallen order, death has standing.

But if one person enters death without sin, without corruption, and without debt to the fallen order, death can touch Him only as an intrusion, not as a lawful possession.

Therefore the resurrection of Christ is not best framed as a random suspension of physics. It is best framed as a structural verdict:

> Death examined Him and found no rightful claim.

The proof is not merely:

> Jesus came back from the dead.

The stronger proof is:

> Death had no rightful jurisdiction over Him, and reality was forced to release Him.

## Formal Shape

Let:

- `D(x)` mean death has rightful jurisdiction over `x`.
- `S(x)` mean `x` bears sin/corruption/debt under the fallen order.
- `C` mean Christ.

The Christian claim has the following jurisdictional structure:

```text
For all x, D(x) only if S(x).
Not S(C).
Therefore not D(C).
```

If death nevertheless receives Christ voluntarily, then death holds what it has no lawful title to retain.

The resurrection is the correction of that unlawful retention.

## What This Does Not Prove Yet

This argument does not, by itself, prove the historical resurrection.

It does not prove from first principles that Jesus of Nazareth is sinless.

It does not prove from physics alone that moral guilt is the ground of death.

It does not prove that biological death is identical to moral jurisdiction.

It proves a conditional structure:

> If the Christian premises are true, resurrection is not an arbitrary miracle. It is the necessary release of the one person death cannot lawfully bind.

That conditional is the testable formal nugget.

## First Weak Point Found

The sentence "sin gives death jurisdiction" must be handled carefully.

If stated too broadly, it appears false or confused, because animals, plants, bodies, and physical systems undergo decay without being moral agents in the same sense humans are.

The better claim is narrower:

> Sin gives death rightful retentive jurisdiction over moral agents.

That distinguishes:

- biological mortality
- creation-wide futility/decay
- moral death
- death's right to retain a person under judgment

Christ can enter biological death voluntarily without death having rightful retentive jurisdiction over Him.

This is the sharper test:

```text
Death can receive Christ by voluntary entry.
Death cannot retain Christ by rightful claim.
```

The post-test version must preserve this distinction.

## Why This Matters

This reframes resurrection away from spectacle and toward authority.

Power can imitate miracle.

Power can violate a boundary.

Power can overwhelm a system.

But rightful authority is different.

The highest test is not whether someone can break a law. The highest test is whether the law itself has to confess that it has no claim over Him.

## Adversarial Questions

These are the first questions the test must survive:

1. Is death being used in two incompatible senses, biological and moral-jurisdictional?
2. Can the premise "sin gives death jurisdiction" be grounded canonically rather than asserted?
3. Does the argument depend on proving Christ's sinlessness from outside Christian revelation?
4. Does "reality was forced to release Him" overstate the case if the event is historical rather than deductive?
5. Can the formal version avoid pretending that Lean proves the historical resurrection?
6. Does the model distinguish resurrection from resuscitation, immortality, simulation rollback, and brute-force rule violation?
7. Does the argument identify a unique Christian structure, or could other traditions claim a similar exception?

## Current Best Wording

The cleanest public form is:

> A miracle proves less than a rightful exception. God is not shown when a rule is merely broken, but when the deepest rule of the fallen world meets the one person it has no authority to bind.

The short form:

> Death held everyone because everyone belonged to its jurisdiction. Christ entered death voluntarily. Death found no claim. Death lost.

## Next Test Layer

The next layer should be formalized as a finite jurisdiction model:

- death jurisdiction
- sin/corruption/debt condition
- biological death vs moral-retentive jurisdiction
- voluntary entry into death
- unlawful retention
- rightful release
- resurrection distinguished from resuscitation
- adversarial alternatives rejected

The goal is not to prove the whole Christian faith in Lean.

The goal is to prove exactly what the model says:

> Under the Christian jurisdictional premises, resurrection is a lawful release, not an arbitrary exception.
