What the English Language Knows About God

A WordNet semantic analysis of moral vocabulary structure

We asked the English language a question it wasn't designed to answer. Princeton's WordNet database — built by linguists cataloging how English speakers actually use words — contains the semantic structure of the entire moral vocabulary. We ran the framework terms through it. The language answered.

Every result below is reproducible. Anyone with Python, NLTK, and the WordNet corpus gets the same numbers. No AI interpretation. No theological argument. Just lexical measurement.

1. Justice and Mercy Are Distant

The first thing the language tells you is that justice and mercy don't naturally sit together.

Wu-Palmer Semantic Similarity
Justice ↔ Mercy: 0.200
On a scale of 0 to 1, where 1 means identical meaning, justice and mercy score 0.200. They are semantically distant. The English language treats them as fundamentally different kinds of concepts that require something external to bring them together.

Compare this to terms that do cluster naturally:

Grace ↔ Coherence: 0.667 — structurally linked
Grace ↔ Love: 0.667 — identical score
Love ↔ Joy: 0.857 — strongest fruit connection
Good ↔ Kindness: 0.923 — deeply connected

Justice ↔ Mercy: 0.200 — distant

Justice and mercy aren't enemies. They're strangers. The language knows they need a bridge.

2. The Gap Nobody Can Cross

Both mercy and coherence need a mediator — a cost-bearer, an intermediary who can bridge the distance between justice and restoration. But neither one can reach it.

Distance to "Mediator"
Mercy → Mediator: 0.154
Dead last. Out of 60+ moral vocabulary terms, "mediator" is the single most distant word from "mercy." The concept mercy needs most is the concept it can reach least.
Distance to "Mediator"
Coherence → Mediator: 0.154
Same score. Coherence — the integration of all things into a unified whole — also cannot reach the mediator. The resolution mechanism is structurally absent from both concepts that require it.

3. Jesus Bridges the Gap

WordNet catalogues Jesus with these semantic identities: Savior, Redeemer, Deliverer, Good Shepherd. Every name is a cost-bearer term. And when we measure his semantic distance to the words mercy and coherence cannot reach:

Distance to "Mediator"
Jesus → Mediator: 0.632
Strong connection. The gap that mercy cannot cross (0.154), Jesus crosses (0.632). The difference is 0.478 — nearly half the entire semantic scale.
Mercy → Mediator: 0.154 (can't reach)
Coherence → Mediator: 0.154 (can't reach)
Jesus → Mediator: 0.632 (bridges the gap)

Jesus connects equally to justice and love:

Jesus ↔ Justice: 0.667
Jesus ↔ Love: 0.667
Jesus ↔ Faith: 0.632

Equal distance to both sides. Connected to the mediator that nothing else reaches. That's not theology — it's lexical measurement.

4. Where Jesus and Death Meet

Death reaches toward the problem: decay (0.857), collapse (0.769), debt (0.667). Jesus reaches toward the solution: mediator (0.632), justice (0.667), burden (0.500). They operate in opposite directions across the moral vocabulary.

But at one word, they score identically:

The Meeting Point
Jesus ↔ Love: 0.667 = Death ↔ Love: 0.667
Love is equidistant from Jesus and death. They meet there. The cost gets paid at the intersection.

5. The Positive Comes First

We tested twenty pairs of positive and negative terms. In every case where an asymmetric relationship exists, the direction is the same: the positive defines the negative. Never reversed.

Light → Darkness = "absence of light"
Life → Death = "departure from life"
Hope → Despair = "all hope is lost or absent"
God → Atheism = "the doctrine that there is no God"
Courage → Cowardice = "the trait of lacking courage"
Moral → Immoral = "not adhering to moral principles"
Coherence → Incoherence = "lack of cohesion"

Zero reversals. Not one negative term defines its positive. The language itself encodes ontological priority: the good, the true, the living — these come first. Their opposites are defined as absences, departures, losses, and lacks.

6. Evil Borrows From Good

We measured how broadly "good" and "evil" connect to the full moral vocabulary — both positive and negative terms.

Semantic Reach
Good differentiates. Evil doesn't.
Good's average similarity to moral terms: 0.585. To immoral terms: 0.492. Gap: 0.093. Good knows the difference between its territory and evil's.

Evil's average similarity to moral terms: 0.559. To immoral terms: 0.557. Gap: 0.002. Evil doesn't distinguish. It reaches into good's vocabulary just as much as its own. Evil has no semantic territory that isn't borrowed from good.

7. The Decoherence Cascade

We traced chains of descent from positive to negative. Each step was measured for semantic similarity. The pattern was consistent: the chain accelerates.

Life → Nihilism (decoherence cascade)
0.727 Life → Death
0.857 Death → Decay
0.909 Decay → Corruption
0.588 Corruption → Nihilism

Each link is stronger than the last — until the final landing. Once life departs, the descent through death, decay, and corruption accelerates before arriving at nihilism. The cascade doesn't slow down. It picks up speed.

Grace → Domination (the resolution problem)
0.364 Grace → Judgment
0.714 Judgment → Punishment
0.588 Punishment → Vengeance
0.588 Vengeance → Domination

Grace has to cross a weak bridge (0.364) to reach judgment. But once in the judgment space, the path to vengeance and domination is strong and uninterrupted. Without a cost-bearer, justice falls into vengeance by semantic gravity.

8. Sacrifice Is the Hub

Across all matrices, one word connects to more resolution concepts than any other: sacrifice. It reaches punishment (0.714), redemption (0.714), reconciliation (0.714), restoration (0.714), cost (0.714), and repair (0.625). Sacrifice is the structural center of the resolution vocabulary — the word that links the problem to the answer.

9. Atheism Is a Religious Orientation

WordNet classifies atheism as IS-A "religious orientation." Not a scientific position. Not a philosophical method. A religious orientation. The language categorizes atheism as the same type of thing as faith — with the opposite answer.

And their semantic profiles are nearly identical:

atheism faith
Love: 0.714 0.714 — equal
Meaning: 0.714 0.714 — equal
Purpose: 0.714 0.714 — equal
Life: 0.714 0.714 — equal
Hope: 0.667 0.800 — faith reaches further
Coherence:0.308 0.500 — faith reaches further
Justice: 0.429 0.800 — faith reaches further
Mediator: 0.143 0.143 — neither reaches

Atheism and faith care about the same things: love, meaning, purpose, life. But faith reaches further into justice (0.800 vs 0.429), coherence (0.500 vs 0.308), and hope (0.800 vs 0.667). Neither one reaches the mediator — only Jesus does.

This is what the data shows. The structures match or they don't. Every number is reproducible by anyone with Python and NLTK. The WordNet database was built by Princeton University linguists cataloging how English speakers use words. No theological argument was embedded in the data.

What you do with it — what it means, what it implies, whether it matters — that's the one variable in the equation that no one else gets to set for you.

Framework, theological claims, and final interpretive responsibility: David Lowe. Mathematical analysis and infrastructure: AI-assisted under David Lowe's direction. Semantic data source: Princeton University WordNet 3.1.

Methodology: Wu-Palmer semantic similarity via NLTK WordNet interface. All comparisons use the best-match across the first three synsets of each word pair. Scores range from 0 (no taxonomic relationship) to 1 (identical meaning). Privation analysis checks whether definitions contain the positive term or privation markers (absence of, lack of, without, not, departure from). Asymmetric dependence checks whether the positive term's definition references the negative.

Reproducibility: pip install nltk && python -c "import nltk; nltk.download('wordnet')" — then run any comparison with wn.synsets(word)[0].wup_similarity(wn.synsets(other)[0])
