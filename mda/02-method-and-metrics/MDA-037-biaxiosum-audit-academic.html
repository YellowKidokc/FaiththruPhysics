The Founders Knew

A Biaxiosum Reading of the American Founding

Prior to the constitutional architecture, prior to the nation itself, there was an epistemological declaration.

"We hold these truths to be self-evident."

The phrase warrants close attention.

The Founders did not claim empirical demonstration ("We have proven these truths through observation"), nor probabilistic evidence ("The data suggests these truths"), nor peer validation ("Published literature supports these truths").

They asserted: These truths are self-evident. A category of epistemic justification that does not reduce to empirical verification but rather constitutes its precondition.

This constitutes Biaxiosum in its classical form: epistemological commitment declared prior to the analytical or political project that flows from it. The Founders presented their metaphysical priors — Creator-endowed rights, natural law, popular sovereignty — before asking the nation to stand on those foundations.

The Four Axioms of American Biaxiosum

The Founders did not relegate their metaphysical commitments to footnotes or appendices — the standard modern academic practice for concealing priors. They placed them in the first paragraph and made them the logical foundation upon which all subsequent political architecture depended.

Axiom 1 — The Source

"...that all men are created equal, that they are endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights..."

The Source is named. Not "endowed by the State." Not "endowed by consensus." Not "endowed by evolutionary advantage." By their Creator.

They told you their bias. They believed in God. They believed rights came from something higher than government.

The Academy's Meta-Bias

Empirical research in social psychology and philosophy of science demonstrates that the institutions most insistent on their own objectivity are structurally least capable of detecting their operative biases.

1

The Bias Blind Spot (BBS)

The Bias Blind Spot (Pronin et al., 2002) produces a systematic asymmetry: individuals identify bias in others at a rate 2x higher than self-detected bias. The effect is amplified among domain experts: senior scientists report "negligible bias" at twice the rate of junior scientists despite equivalent measured bias. (Nature, 2021)

2

Naive Realism

Naive Realism (Ross & Ward, 1996): the epistemological error of assuming one's own perception is unmediated access to reality, such that disagreement is attributable only to the other party's bias rather than to divergent prior commitments.

3

The Introspection Illusion

The Introspection Illusion (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977): the systematic overweighting of phenomenological self-reports ("I feel objective") relative to behavioral and third-party evidence of actual bias.

4

Ideological Homogeneity

Structural ideological homogeneity in academia violates Hull's diversity-of-bias principle: elite liberal arts institutions report Democrat-to-Republican faculty ratios of 12.7:1 (Langbert, 2018); sociology departments report ratios approaching 108:0. Under such conditions, the self-correcting mechanism of science — which requires diversity of bias, not absence of bias — is structurally unavailable.

The Hull Principle

Philosopher of science David Hull (1988) argued that scientific progress requires not the absence of bias in individual scientists but the diversity of bias across the scientific community. When biases cluster homogeneously — as the data above documents — the adversarial structure that enables error-correction collapses. The result is a closed epistemic system in which consensus and correctness become indistinguishable.

A recursive epistemic failure: claimed objectivity becomes the mechanism of ideological capture's concealment.
