# Network Science :: Ecclesiology

## DOMAINS

**Domain A — Network Science**
Scale-free networks exhibit power-law degree distributions: most nodes have few connections, a few hubs have many. Barabasi and Albert (1999) showed these emerge via preferential attachment — new nodes connect preferentially to well-connected nodes. Small-world networks (Watts & Strogatz 1998) have short average path lengths despite high clustering. Hubs are simultaneously the network's greatest strength (efficiency) and greatest vulnerability (targeted attack).

**Domain B — Theology**
The early church grew from 120 (Acts 1:15) to millions within three centuries. Matthew 25:29: "For to everyone who has, more shall be given, and he will have an abundance" — a statement of preferential attachment ~1,950 years before Barabasi formalized it. The church's structure in Acts shows clustering (house churches), hub nodes (apostles, Paul), weak ties (missionary journeys), and resilience through decentralization (persecution scattered but didn't destroy the network).

## TESTS

**Swap Test**: PASSED. Replace "hub node" with "apostle" and "preferential attachment" with "Matthew 25:29" — the network dynamics are identical. The early church's growth pattern is empirically scale-free, not random.

**Bidirectional Prediction**: Network science predicts that removing hubs should cause maximum damage → Acts records that persecution targeted leaders (James, Peter, Paul). Theology predicts that scattering should spread the message → network science confirms that distributing nodes creates new clusters (Acts 8:4).

**Falsification Condition**: If church growth patterns followed random rather than scale-free dynamics — i.e., if every believer had roughly the same number of connections and influence — the mapping would collapse. Historical evidence (Paul's networks, Acts growth data) shows clear power-law structure.
