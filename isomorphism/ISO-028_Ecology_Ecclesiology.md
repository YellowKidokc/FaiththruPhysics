# ISO-028: Ecology ↔ Ecclesiology — The Church as Ecosystem

## DOMAINS

**Domain A — Ecology**
Ecosystems are characterized by: biodiversity (Shannon Diversity Index: H' = -Σ pᵢ ln pᵢ), keystone species (disproportionate impact relative to abundance), ecological succession (pioneer → intermediate → climax community), resilience (ability to absorb disturbance and reorganize), invasive species (displace native species, reduce diversity), and symbiosis (mutualistic, commensal, or parasitic interactions). The species-area relationship (S = cAᶻ) describes how species count scales with habitat size.

**Domain B — Theology (Ecclesiology)**
The church is described as a body with many members (1 Cor 12:12-27): diverse gifts, interdependent functions, no part dispensable. Apostolic function (Eph 4:11-13) serves a disproportionate structural role. Church planting follows a succession pattern (pioneer → established → sending). Persecution tests resilience. False doctrine functions as an invasive species. Fellowship (koinonia) is symbiotic.

## TESTS

**Swap Test**: PASSED for 8/10 correspondences.
- "Remove the keystone species and the ecosystem collapses" → "Remove apostolic function and the church loses structural integrity" — this is a testable ecclesiological claim, consistent with historical observations of churches that lose founding leadership without succession planning.
- "Low biodiversity ecosystems are fragile" → "Low gift-diversity churches are fragile" — predicts that homogeneous congregations (single demographic, single gift emphasis) will be less resilient to disruption. Empirically plausible.
- "Invasive species reduce native diversity" → "False doctrine reduces gift diversity" — predicts that doctrinal error will correlate with functional homogenization (e.g., cults suppress diverse gifts in favor of conformity). Empirically observed.
- Correspondences 9 (nutrient cycling) and 10 (edge effects) are weaker: the mappings are suggestive but do not generate specific predictions that distinguish them from generic metaphor.

**Bidirectional Prediction**:
- Forward (ecology → theology): Ecology predicts that church communities should exhibit a species-area relationship — larger communities should support more distinct ministry functions. This is testable and likely true (small churches cannot sustain the same functional diversity as large ones).
- Reverse (theology → ecology): The theological insistence that "the eye cannot say to the hand, 'I don't need you'" (1 Cor 12:21) predicts functional interdependence in ecosystems — which is a foundational ecological principle.

**Falsification**: If church communities showed no ecological dynamics — no succession patterns, no keystone-species effects, no diversity-resilience correlation, no invasive-species behavior from false doctrine. The mapping would also weaken if ecological principles failed to predict church community behavior in any systematic way.

## CLASSIFICATION

**Rating**: Testing / Medium-high confidence.

**Strength**: 8 of 10 correspondences are robust, generating specific, testable predictions. The keystone species ↔ apostolic function mapping is particularly strong: it predicts measurable structural consequences of leadership loss, which can be tested against church history and sociology of religion data.

**Weakness**: Correspondences 9 and 10 are stretched. Nutrient cycling ↔ resource circulation is a reasonable metaphor but lacks the structural precision of the other mappings. Edge effects ↔ missional engagement is suggestive but too vague to test.

**Novel insight**: The Shannon Diversity Index provides a quantitative framework for ecclesiology. H' = -Σ pᵢ ln pᵢ applied to gift distribution in a congregation would predict resilience — low H' (dominated by one or two gifts) predicts fragility, high H' (evenly distributed gifts) predicts robustness. This is testable with congregational survey data.
