The Empirical Foundation: Structure Before Culture

Before the formal proof begins, two bodies of real-world evidence establish that math and moral structure are not cultural inventions but pre-loaded features of how our minds work — features that appear before language, before schooling, and before society can explain them.

Mathematical Structure Precedes Language

The Approximate Number System (ANS) — our brain's built-in sense of quantity — is present and measurable in babies who can't talk yet. Behavioral and brain studies show that infants as young as 3–5 months can tell the difference between quantities — like 8 dots vs. 16 dots — even when total area, density, and other visual features are controlled. Their discrimination follows Weber's law (the ratio between quantities matters, not the absolute difference). This isn't pattern recognition. This is numerical structure.

The evidence is long-term: ANS sharpness measured at 6 months predicts standardized math scores at 3.5 years. Twin studies at 5 months show the sensitivity is partly inherited. EEG (brain wave) work reveals that 3–4-month-old infants show distinct brain signals when hand-opening actions match or don't match dot arrays — linking number to action before the infant can crawl, let alone count.

What the ANS Data Actually Means

These results mean that human cognitive systems don't invent number. They encounter and approximate a structure that's already working. The most natural reading is that mathematical structure is prior to and independent of specific material configurations and developmental stages — which is exactly what Rules A1–A7 in Book I formalize. The receiver didn't create the signal. The signal was already there.

Moral Evaluation Precedes Socialization

The helper-versus-hinderer experiments (Hamlin et al.) show that 6- and 10-month-old infants preferentially reach for helpful agents over harmful ones in simple climbing scenarios. At 3 and 6 months, infants look significantly longer at helpers than hinderers. Follow-up studies demonstrate that infants distinguish helpers from hinderers even when a neutral character is present — they aren't just tracking novelty but evaluating moral valence (good vs. bad).

By 4–5 years, children not only prefer helpers but call them "nicer," allocate punishment disproportionately to hinderers, and justify those judgments verbally. With simplified procedures, 3-year-olds show the same patterns.

This isn't learned behavior in any straightforward sense. It's moral evaluation emerging from cognitive architecture that precedes the cultural and linguistic systems that supposedly "construct" morality. The moral signal isn't invented by society. It unfolds from structure — which is exactly what Book III formalizes as the visceral response argument and what Rule A11 derives from information theory.
